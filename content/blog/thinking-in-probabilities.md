Most financial advice is stated as certainty: *buy this*, *sell that*, *the market will*. Real decisions, however, are made under uncertainty. The disciplined approach is not to predict the future but to reason about a range of outcomes and their likelihoods.

## The core idea

A financial decision is a bet on the future. Every bet has:

- **Possible outcomes** — the scenarios that could play out.
- **Probabilities** — how likely each scenario is.
- **Payoffs** — what each scenario is worth to you.

The expected value of a decision is the sum of (probability × payoff) across all scenarios. A good process does not guarantee a good outcome in any single case; it guarantees good outcomes over many cases.

## Why this matters

1. **It separates process from result.** A losing trade made with sound reasoning is not a mistake; it is the cost of a correct process. Conflating the two leads to over-trading after luck and paralysis after a loss.
2. **It forces you to name your assumptions.** "The market will rise" is untestable. "Given these earnings and rates, there is a 60% chance this asset appreciates 10%+" is falsifiable.
3. **It manages risk, not just return.** The downside scenario and its probability matter as much as the upside. Surviving the bad cases is what lets you stay in the game for the good ones.

## A practical checklist

- What are the 3–4 realistic scenarios over my horizon?
- What probability would I assign to each, and why?
- What is the payoff in each, including the worst case?
- Does the expected value justify the risk of ruin?
- What would change my mind — what is my exit signal?

## Closing thought

Markets reward the patient and the honest with themselves. If you can hold a probabilistic view without pretending false certainty, you already have an edge over most participants who are certain about nothing they can defend.
